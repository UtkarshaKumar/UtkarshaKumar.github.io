---
title: The Wrong Half of a Good Idea
date: 2026-08-16
---

Airbnb won't show you what a guest wrote about you until you've written about them. You each get [fourteen days after checkout](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/13), and the reviews stay hidden until both are in or the window closes.

That timing is why the reviews are worth reading. Nobody can wait to see what the other person said and answer in kind, so nobody softens a review to avoid getting one back. Take the timing away and you get what most rating systems get: fours and fives, and no information in either direction.

Now picture copying Airbnb. You'd copy stars, photos, a review box, probably host responses. All visible, all easy, all beside the point. The stars are the half you can see. The timing is the half doing the work.

That second half is what actually travels between products, and it's almost never the feature.

Say the rule without naming the company: hold two judgments until both are committed, then reveal them together. Nothing in that sentence is about travel. It describes sealed bids, blind peer review, two sides exchanging salary numbers at once, and every performance review process that quietly fails because the manager writes theirs after reading yours.

So here's a test. Describe the thing without naming the industry it came from. If the description survives, you've found the mechanism, and it will probably work somewhere else. If you can't manage it without saying hosts or listings or tickets, you're holding the surface — and copying that gets you the shape without the result.

## The cost of building used to kill bad copies

Getting this wrong used to be self-limiting. Building was slow, you always had more ideas than engineers, and the weak ones died of neglect before anyone had to argue against them.

That's ending. AI writes the prototype, the tests, and a growing share of the real thing. When building costs almost nothing, "we saw someone else do this" becomes a sufficient reason to ship, and there's no longer a queue for bad ideas to die in. The failure won't look like failure. It'll look like a team shipping constantly and getting nowhere.

Which puts the weight on two things, both of which happen before anyone writes code.

## Without a target behaviour, everything looks worth copying

The first is knowing what behaviour you're trying to cause. Not which feature is missing — what you want people to actually do more of. Airbnb needed strangers to trust each other enough to sleep in each other's houses, and everything about that review rule follows from it. If you can't finish that sentence for your own product, every mechanism you come across will look equally worth having. That's how you end up with a competitor spreadsheet and a product assembled from unrelated parts.

## A working mechanism hides what it needs

The second is knowing what the mechanism needs in order to work at all. Simultaneous reveal only does something where both sides review each other and both can retaliate. Put it in a marketplace where only buyers rate sellers and it protects nobody; it's just a delay. Conditions like these are invisible in the finished product, because by the time you're looking at it they're already met. Airbnb's rule works partly because a fourteen-day deadline is short enough that most people write something, which only holds when both sides have a reason to bother.

Get either part wrong and you'll decide the mechanism was overrated, when what you really did was take the wrong half of it.
