The Loudest Person in the Organization Is Often the Least Reliable Signal

2026-10

A recent post by Nikita Bier shared a clip built around a line attributed to Elon Musk: "The loudest people are the frauds."

The line is deliberately provocative, but it captures a pattern I have seen repeatedly across consulting engagements and product organizations.

The people who understand a system deeply are rarely the loudest people in the room. They ask precise questions, trace problems back to their source, and work through complexity without turning every failure into a performance.

When something breaks, they review the problem with the team. They examine the customer requirement, the product decision, the technical dependency, and the assumptions that led there. They identify next steps, take responsibility for their part, and run an honest postmortem when the solution fails.

They also work closely with the team. They listen to the people closest to the product and the customer. They use that perspective to find a path forward.

That is leadership.

The dangerous person is not simply someone who speaks loudly. Organizations need people who can challenge weak thinking and raise uncomfortable problems. The danger comes from a person whose volume is disconnected from evidence, accountability, and results.

This person turns opinion into fact. Anecdote becomes data. Disagreement becomes proof that everyone else is incompetent. The narrative becomes more important than reality.

At first, this can look like decisive leadership. The person always has an explanation. They always know who is at fault. They always sound certain.

Then the organization starts losing its ability to think.

People who understand the system are ignored, demoted, or pushed out. Yes men are promoted because they do not challenge the story. Teams stop reporting problems honestly. Customer requirements remain wrong because nobody is allowed to question them.

Eventually, the organization can no longer distinguish between a real problem and a loud complaint about a problem.

Leadership should ask:

  • Have they reviewed the problem with the team and identified concrete next steps?
  • How deeply are they connected to the product and customer problem?
  • Have they run a real postmortem when their solution went wrong?
  • Have they worked with the team to understand different perspectives?
  • What have they personally changed in the last six months?
  • Do they set direction and give the team room to work, or do they only shout and blame?

If the answer to most of these questions is no, leadership has found a serious risk.

A real leader sets direction, gives the team room to do the work, and steps in when the team needs help correcting a miss. A performer shouts over the team and treats control as leadership.

The burden of detecting this falls on the leaders managing that person. Ask the team:

  • Would you choose to work with this person again?
  • Can you disagree with them without being punished?
  • Do they change their mind when the evidence changes?
  • Does the team become stronger around them, or more dependent on them?

The answers matter more than the leader's self-assessment.

A person who gathers influence by making everyone else look incompetent can eventually make the entire organization incompetent. Negativity spreads. Fear becomes a management system. The loud person appears validated because the people who disagreed have already left.

Organizations should put problem solvers and collaborators in positions of influence, especially where product and company direction are being set.

The question is not who sounds most confident. It is who sees reality most clearly, takes responsibility when reality goes wrong, and leaves the team better equipped for the next problem.

Loudness is not leadership. It is only volume.
